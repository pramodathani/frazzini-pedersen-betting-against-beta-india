Abstract

1. Introduction

Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) argue that investors who cannot borrow tilt towards high-beta shares to get more return, which bids those shares up and leaves them with lower risk-adjusted returns than the CAPM predicts. A factor that is long leveraged low-beta shares and short high-beta shares, scaled so that it has zero beta, should then earn a positive return. They find that it does in US stocks from 1926 to 2012, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.78, and in 19 other developed markets, Treasuries, credit and futures. India does not appear in their paper.

This companion applies their construction to Indian shares. The controls above choose the universe and whether Indian trading costs are deducted; every table and figure in Sections 3 to 5 responds to them. All returns are monthly and in excess of the risk-free rate unless stated, and every t-statistic uses Newey–West standard errors.

2. Data and method

We use NSE's daily price files from 1995 to October 2026, which hold every share while it traded, including shares later delisted, with returns adjusted for splits, bonuses and dividends from NSE's corporate action list, which is complete from 2005. Funds, exchange traded funds and rights entitlements are removed, and a share that is renamed is linked to its earlier history by its ISIN code. The market is the NIFTY 500 total-return index and the risk-free rate is the RBI 91-day Treasury bill yield.

Following the paper, the beta of a share is the correlation of its overlapping three-day log returns with the market over five years, times the ratio of its one-year volatility to the market's, shrunk towards one with a weight of 0.6. Betas need at least 750 daily observations, so the first portfolio forms in January 2008. At each month-end shares are sorted on beta, and the portfolio is held for the next month. Deciles use cut points set by the 500 most traded shares, which plays the role of the paper's NYSE breakpoints. The BAB factor weights each half of the shares by the rank of its beta, scales each half to a beta of one, and goes long the low-beta half and short the high-beta half.

All backtests use the brahmagupta engine with NSE delivery costs: securities transaction tax, stamp duty, exchange and SEBI charges and GST, plus an assumed market impact of 0.10% a side for the 500 most traded shares and 0.30% for all shares. Because the short side cannot be held overnight in Indian cash shares, and no borrowing fee is charged, the BAB factor is a research portfolio, not an investable one.

5. Where the premium comes from

The low-beta leg carries the premium: its 4-factor alpha is far above the high-beta leg's, as in the paper's Table B1 (0.36% against −0.01%).

Before costs the premium is largest in the two least traded groups and weaker in the others, which fits the finding that dropping the least traded half of shares cuts the all-share BAB by half (Table 7).

8. Limits

The Indian sample has 224 monthly returns against about 1,000 in the paper's US sample, so a Sharpe ratio of 0.78 would give a t-statistic near 3.4 here instead of 7. We tried 17 specifications; the main one was fixed before any result was seen, and with a Bonferroni correction for 17 tests only the all-share BAB before costs clears the 5% bar. There is no market capitalisation before 2024, so traded value stands in for size and for value weights. There is no Indian liquidity factor, factor alphas cover 215 months because the IIMA factors end in December 2025, and the paper's funding-liquidity tests (TED spread) are not replicated. NSE's files do not say what holders received when a share was delisted, so the study tests losses of 30% and 100% at delisting. An adversarial review of the code and the data found no look-ahead or coding errors, and its fixes are in the repository.
